Study: Adopting an AI documentation assistant in primary care
Participant P05, family physician, Northgate Community Clinic

Interviewer: I understand you've decided not to use the documentation assistant regularly. Can you tell me about that decision?

Participant: Sure. I tried it for about three weeks, properly, in every visit where the patient agreed. Then I stopped. It wasn't one thing, it was an accumulation.

Interviewer: What were the things?

Participant: First, accuracy. I found errors in roughly one note out of five. Small ones mostly, a dose recorded wrong, laterality flipped, left knee instead of right. But one note said I'd examined a patient's abdomen when I hadn't. That's a falsified record if I sign it without noticing. The software doesn't get tired, but it also doesn't know when it's guessing.

Interviewer: How much time did checking take?

Participant: That's the second thing. To check a note properly, I have to remember the visit in detail and compare it line by line. That took me about as long as writing a short note myself. So for me the time savings didn't materialise. Maybe if you write very long notes it's different. My notes are short.

Interviewer: Some colleagues describe saving a lot of time.

Participant: I believe them, and I don't think they're wrong to use it. But I also think some of them are reading less carefully than they think they are. That's not a criticism of them, it's human nature. If something is right ninety percent of the time, your attention drifts. Our regulatory college is clear that the physician is responsible for the content of the record, no matter who or what drafted it.

Interviewer: You mentioned an accumulation. What else?

Participant: Training residents. We supervise family medicine residents here. Writing a note is how residents learn to organise their thinking: what's the problem, what's the evidence, what's the plan. If the machine writes the note, what happens to that skill? I've asked our residents not to use it during their first year with us, and our program director supports that for now.

Interviewer: How have patients responded when you did use it?

Participant: Most didn't mind. A couple of my older patients were quite pleased, actually, they said it was nice to see me not typing. One patient asked me directly whether the company would use her conversation to improve its product, and I couldn't give her a confident answer at the time. That bothered me. I don't like telling patients things I'm not sure about.

Interviewer: Did you raise those concerns with the clinic?

Participant: I did, at a staff meeting and in writing to our executive director. To her credit she took it seriously and asked the health authority for clearer answers about data use. We got a better document a month later. But the decision to join the pilot had already been made above our heads, so it felt like we were fixing the plane while flying it.

Interviewer: Is there anything that would change your mind?

Participant: A few things. A proper connection to our record system so I'm not copying and pasting. Evidence from independent evaluations, not just the vendor's numbers, about error rates. And a clear way to see which parts of the note came from the conversation and which parts the software inferred. If it highlighted what it wasn't sure about, I'd trust it more, not less.

Interviewer: That's a helpful suggestion.

Participant: I'm not against the technology. I'm against using it without knowing how good it is. If someone showed me that it was as accurate as a good human scribe, I'd use it tomorrow.
